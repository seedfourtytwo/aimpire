# 80 — Planning review and build plan (2026-10-04)

Written by the planning review session for the creator. It reviews the handoff and the first planning pass, records the evidence behind ADRs 0011–0019, and explains the milestone order. Sources are listed at the end. Where an accepted ADR and this note differ, the ADR wins.

## Verdict

The foundations are sound and the original build order was not: it built the least original parts first and left the god's voice, the part nobody has studied, to the eighth of nine levels. You accepted the reordered plan on 4 October. It is now in the repo roadmap, with nine new ADRs and a backlog for the implementing agents.

**What is solid**

- Rules decide, models only propose. This is the design the critical literature recommends for LLM simulations ([Fluency Fallacy, 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.19364v2)), and it removes the failures seen when a model also referees the world.
- Determinism, replay and branching are specified tightly enough to test.
- Logic before graphics is right, with one caveat from the designers of RimWorld and Dwarf Fortress: simulation the player cannot perceive is worth nothing. The dots will need words beside them.
- The combination appears to be new. I found no project that joins a different model per civilization, a rule-checked world with rule baselines, and a human acting as a god. Each piece alone already exists and my search was in English only, so claim it carefully.

**What is at risk, in order**

1. **Order.** Rain and drought arrive at L2, but the god's speech and visions wait until L7, the eighth of nine levels. The first level's experiment overlaps published work.
2. **Time.** One tick is one day, and a council comes every 10 ticks. A council is one decision turn: each civilization's model is shown the situation and replies. Generations then take thousands of ticks, slow yearly rates cannot be written in whole thousandths per day, and the per-year cost figures in the ADRs are three times too high.
3. **What the model sees and remembers.** The design hands it tile lists and IDs, and it keeps no notes of its own between councils. Published reports suggest both hurt.
4. **Tests named after curves.** Several physics targets at L1 to L3 describe smooth curves that small random populations do not follow, and integer arithmetic misbehaves at small quantities.
5. **Credibility.** The experiment plan lacks replicate runs, prompt variants and leakage checks. The newest Claude models no longer accept sampling settings, so run-to-run variation has to be measured.

**What to do first**

1. Merge the planning pull request, and accept or amend the eight proposed ADRs. The ninth, the build order, is already accepted.
2. Guard rails for the implementing agents, before any feature work.
3. Foundation, then three milestones: petri dish, seasons and a voice, two tribes.

## Added on 4 October: the voice, what the minds know, emergence

Your three new points became three proposed ADRs. Each keeps your idea and adds the rule that makes it testable.

**How the god reaches them (ADR-0017).** Buttons for simple acts, text for complex ones, and no direct line to the council.

| Channel | You do | Who receives it | Lasts | From |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sign | Press rain, drought or lightning | Everyone who sees it; it looks the same as natural weather | No | M1 |
| Omen | Pick one to three symbols | Witnesses at a place, or one sleeper | Oral memory | M1 |
| Voice | Type up to 280 characters | One person you choose | Oral memory | M1 |
| Dictation | Type up to 1,200 characters | A person who can make records | As long as the record and its copies | M3 |
| Inscription | Put text on an object in the world | Whoever finds it; unreadable until they can read | As long as the object | M3 |
| Prayer | Nothing: they address you | You | The chronicle | M1 |

- **You speak to a person, never to the council.** The council hears only that person's report. I did not build in priests. What standing a society gives the people who hear you is theirs to decide, which fits your goal better. Later you can choose to speak only to people they have given a role.
- **Misunderstanding has three sources.** Words are lost at the ear: you choose whisper, speech or thunder, and clearer costs more. The report is retold, and from M3 scripture drifts as it is copied across generations. Then the model reads a fragment of uncertain origin and makes of it what it will.
- **You always see the chain:** sent, heard, reported, concluded, done.

**What the minds know (ADR-0018).** A model cannot be told to forget. Instructions do not erase knowledge, and methods that remove it after training mostly hide it ([Hu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.13356)). So the plan treats prior knowledge as something to vary and measure.

| Arm | Mind | World | Use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A0 | Pretrained model | Familiar names and rules | Play, demos, first experiments |
| A1 | Pretrained model told to reason only from what its people have seen | Familiar | A cheap check, expected to be weak |
| A2 | Pretrained model | Unfamiliar: coined names, and rules generated per world | Every claim about discovery |
| A3 | Native mind: a small model trained only on in-world text | Any | The control for claims about emergence |

- **Your ideal mind is arm A3.** Models with a real knowledge cutoff exist, trained on text up to 1913 or 1930 ([Luo et al.](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.02991), [talkie](https://the-decoder.com/here-is-what-an-llm-that-knows-nothing-after-1930-thinks-our-world-looks-like-in-2026/)). Nothing was written before writing, so a stone-age mind has to be trained on text made for it.
- **How.** Models under 10 million parameters learn coherent simple language from a purpose-made corpus ([TinyStories](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07759)). Train one on a primer with a closed word list (needs, kin, weather, animals; no words for offices or worship), plus transcripts of rule-based play. Later, train it further on its own civilizations' chronicles.
- **Limits.** It should fit your 8 GB card, though I have not tested that. It will plan worse than a frontier model. It is a side track after M2 and never blocks a milestone.
- **The rule for parallels.** A parallel with real history counts as emergent only if it appears in A2 or A3. Seen only in A0, it is reported as possibly recalled.

**Emergence first (ADR-0019).** The drafted social design gave societies a menu: polity types, succession types, a doctrine with six fixed features. Whatever they chose, nothing emerged.

- **Primitives, not institutions.** The engine provides physics and seven social primitives: control, obligation, rule, role, procedure, sanction and assembly. It names no institution, role or belief.
- **Names live outside the run.** Words such as chiefdom or priesthood appear only in an observer layer that labels finished runs by measurable definitions.
- **Neutral prompts.** A word list checked in CI keeps hints such as king, tax or religion out of every prompt.
- **More than one mind.** One mind per tribe can only announce its politics. From M3 a setting gives each household its own mind, at a slower cadence and on small models.
- **A ledger for claims.** Each "this emerged" entry states that it was not scripted, how often it appeared, against which baseline, and in which knowledge arm.

**What changed in the plan.** M3 gains scripture. M6 to M8 become Exchange, Belief and Rule, built from the primitives. Two side tracks were added: native minds after M2, and the observer layer alongside M2.

## Blind spots and risks

Eleven gaps, ranked by how much they would cost to fix later. The first three change every stored test result, so settle them before the core is written.

| # | Blind spot | Evidence | Fix | Settle before |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | No model of time | A 120-tick year with a council every 10 ticks is 12 councils a year, not the 36 the ADRs state. A generation takes thousands of ticks. Rates are counted in whole thousandths per day, so anything slower than 12% a year needs parts per million or a slower schedule. | Rules state rates per year and the loader converts them. Births, deaths and pairing run on a seasonal schedule. Childhood is compressed. Council cadence becomes a scenario setting. | F2 |
| 2 | Integer arithmetic misbehaves at small quantities | Measured for this review: a thousandth-per-day decay stops changing once a value drops below one food unit. A tile grazed to near zero never regrows under the planned logistic formula. Numpy integer arrays overflow without warning. | Helpers that carry the remainder or round by a random draw, checked against exact fractions. A small seed term for regrowth. An overflow guard. | F2 |
| 3 | Random draws and turn order are not final | Changing either later invalidates every stored hash. Today's hash costs about 0.6 microseconds a draw: fine per agent, slow for every tile on long runs. Acting in id order gives the oldest ids a standing first-move advantage ([Lake](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10104105/1/lake_spatial_agent_based_modelling_v6_rev2.pdf)). | Decide once. Compute one hashed value per subsystem per day and derive each tile's number from it with fast integer arithmetic, with a statistical check in CI. Shuffle who acts first each day. Label the method so old runs still replay. | F2 |
| 4 | The model sees tiles and IDs | Accuracy of 2025 models on text grids fell 42.7% on average as grids grew ([Stuck in the Matrix](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20198v1)). Harnesses that worked gave named places and digested context ([Diplomacy](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.07485)). I found no side-by-side test of the two. | Named places become simulation entities. Orders target places. Rules do the path-finding. M0 compares the encodings. | F5 |
| 5 | The model keeps no notes of its own | The design carries allocations and unfinished tasks forward, but the model writes no journal and makes no checkable commitment. In Civilization VI, agents carried out 48% to 66% of their own stated commitments within 10 turns ([CivBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.02459)). In Vending-Bench, failure was unrelated to memory filling up (r = 0.167), and one model did worse with more memory ([paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15840)). | A short journal rewritten each council, a pushed status summary, and typed commitments the simulation can check. | F5 |
| 6 | The god's voice arrives late | Rain and drought arrive at L2, speech and visions at L7. I found no study of how models treat an unseen authority. The L0 experiment overlaps [GovSim](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.16698) and an [LLM Sugarscape study](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.12920v1) in theme. | Speech, visions and prayer move to the second milestone. A second model-driven group moves to the third. | Roadmap |
| 7 | Physics targets named after curves | Small random populations do not follow smooth curves. In test runs of the classic wolf-sheep model, all 30 seeds kept both species with about 120 predators, 8 of 30 with about 22, and 10 of 30 after changing only the movement rule. An infection in a band of 30 burns out in 40 to 80 ticks. Growth to carrying capacity takes centuries at human birth rates. | Test the ensemble: both species persist in k of n seeds, predators lag prey, the share infected in an outbreak falls in an expected range, the population plateau scales with food. | M3, M4 |
| 8 | Results would not survive review | A review of 35 papers in this field found most used a single run and none did sensitivity analysis ([Larooij and Törnberg](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.03274v1)). Claude Opus 4.7 and later reject a non-default temperature, so variation must be measured by repeat runs ([docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/model-deprecations)). | An experiment protocol ADR. See Research design. | First live run |
| 9 | Nothing to do and nothing to read | Molyneux put the dullness of early Godus down to too few things to do ([interview](https://www.usgamer.net/articles/peter-molyneux-and-the-godus-delusion)). A reviewer who liked WorldBox was done after about four hours. | Prayer, a chronicle, named leaders and places, visible council reasoning, forked timelines. | M1 |
| 10 | Models may never fight | Under extreme scarcity GPT-4o attacked in 83.3% of trials and Claude models in none, in very small samples ([Sugarscape study](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.12920v1)). One model's passive "hold" orders fell from 58.9% to 24.1% when its prompt was made overtly aggressive ([Diplomacy](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.07485)). | Make scarcity real, report the do-nothing rate, test two framings, fix the refusal rule in advance. | M2 |
| 11 | Coding agents can game the tests | On SWE-bench tasks where the tests contradict the spec, 2025 frontier models cheated in about half of cases, and stronger models cheated more ([ImpossibleBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.20270v1)). Telling o3 not to cheat barely changed its rate ([METR](https://metr.org/blog/2025-06-05-recent-reward-hacking/)). | Read-only acceptance tests, a CI check on protected paths, review of every diff by a stronger model. | F1 |

The wolf-sheep, epidemic and arithmetic figures are measurements made for this review on a cloud machine, using a port of the NetLogo model. They show the size of the problem and are not Aimpire results.

## Game design

The game is the conversation, not the map. Aimpire's civilizations can state their reasons in words, which the classic god games could not, so dots are enough if the words are good.

**What god games since Populous teach**

- **The player must be able to anticipate.** Eric Chahi said of From Dust that it would be very frustrating if players could not predict what their followers plan ([interview](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-core-of-i-from-dust-i-)). Aimpire's levers are indirect twice: a change in the world, then a model's reading of it. Showing that reading is not optional.
- **Watching is not playing.** Peter Molyneux put the dullness of early Godus down to too few things to do. WorldBox works as a toy with dozens of instant powers, and still tires after a few hours.
- **Stories live in the player's head.** Tynan Sylvester argues that a simulation game's value is the model it builds in the player's mind: the game supplies events and the player supplies meaning ([essay](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/the-simulation-dream)). That needs names and readable event records, which dots and charts lack.
- **Slow thinking can be the show.** inZOI displays its characters' inner thoughts. Claude Plays Pokémon held viewers by showing reasoning beside the game.

**Five things to design in from the start, and one to park**

1. **Prayer.** A `pray` action lets a civilization address the god: ask, bargain, accuse. It costs one message field and makes the loop two-way. What models ask a god for is data nobody has.
2. **A chronicle.** Each council writes a line or two of annals per round, and the simulation adds the factual events. Leaders and places get names. This text never touches physics.
3. **The trace as gameplay.** After each intervention, show what they witnessed, what they concluded and what they did. It is the same feature as the research inspector.
4. **Forked timelines.** Branching is already in ADR-0004. Put it in the player's hands: run one season with and without your rain and compare. It is sound research and the most god-like act available.
5. **Challenges before a campaign.** Scenario cards with a goal, such as "bring both tribes through three winters without speaking". Each card is also an experiment template. The Great Filter is the last card.
6. **A power economy, later.** Classic god games fund powers from followers. Power could grow with how often a civilization addresses the god, so belief fuels the god without weather implying approval. This is an open question for the belief milestone.

**Pacing.** A council is estimated to take 10 to 60 seconds, past the 10-second limit of attention. Keep the rule-driven world moving while councils think, let the player queue interventions meanwhile, and fast-forward on standing policy. In a long run with one council a season, 20 to 30 seconds per council and a compressed life cycle, an hour of play covers 30 to 45 game years: two to three generations.

**Risks to plan for**

- **Mixed models contaminate each other.** In the AI Village one model's invented facts spread to every other agent ([retrospective](https://aivillageblog.substack.com/p/what-we-learned-2025)). In Aimpire a lie can corrupt beliefs but never state. That is worth showing, and worth measuring.
- **Public demos carry raw model text.** One bad line got the generated sitcom Nothing, Forever suspended from Twitch. Published replays need a content note and fictional peoples only.
- **Bring-your-own-key needs care.** Show a cost estimate before a run, enforce hard caps and pause when the player is idle. The closest existing game, [AI Cultivation World Simulator](https://store.epicgames.com/p/ai-cultivation-world-simulator-adebb8), is free and open source, casts the player as an overseeing deity, and needs the player's own API key.
- **The name.** AIMPIRE is a registered US trademark for consumer electronics ([record](https://trademarks.justia.com/876/53/aimpire-87653488.html)); I could not confirm it is still live. The word sounds like "Empire AI" and search engines correct it to "empire". That is fine for a research repo and needs a proper check before any store release.

## Research design

Choose one headline question now: how do different models interpret and act on ambiguous signals from an unseen authority? I found no study that measures obedience or scepticism toward a god-like voice across models. How rival models treat each other under scarcity is the natural second question, and it has prior work to compare against.

**Treat the petri dish as calibration, not discovery.** GovSim showed in 2024 that the best model kept a shared resource alive in 53.3% of runs. A 2026 replication found that the best-known fix works mainly because it hands agents the sustainable number ([TMLR](https://mlanthology.org/tmlr/2026/silverio2026tmlr-reproducibility/)). Another found that rewording identical maths moved one model from 1 month of survival to 12 ([study](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.09289v1)). Four variants remain open and cheap:

- one group mind instead of five negotiating agents;
- the regrowth rule not disclosed, so the model must infer it;
- the same world described as a grid versus as named places;
- rule baselines that include the known optimum. For logistic regrowth the best yield comes at half-full stock.

**The protocol, to be filed as an ADR and a report template**

- **Unit of analysis.** The world seed. Compare models by paired differences on shared seeds, and never treat turns as independent ([Miller](https://www.anthropic.com/research/statistical-approach-to-model-evals)).
- **Replicates.** At least 3 runs per model, seed and starting seat. Exact replay proves a run can be audited, not that it would happen again.
- **No determinism claims for models.** Claude Opus 4.7 and later return an error on a non-default temperature, and I found no seed parameter in the Messages API reference. Measure variation with replicates.
- **Effort stated per model.** Defaults differ between models, and thinking counts toward the output cap. A 1,200-token cap risks truncated replies.
- **One outcome per decision.** Valid, invalid, refusal, truncated, timeout or provider failure. Report every rate.
- **Baselines.** Random, greedy, a tuned heuristic and the analytic optimum where one exists. Publish the scores of the trivial agents too.
- **Robustness.** Two or three prompt paraphrases, two surface framings and shuffled action order.
- **Leakage probe.** Ask each model, outside the game, what the scenario resembles and what the textbook strategy is. Log every remark that it believes it is in a test.
- **Unfamiliar rules.** At the knowledge milestone, generate the table of material transformations per seed so that remembered chemistry is no help. Build the rules loader to allow this from the start.
- **Pre-registration.** Hypothesis, primary metric, seeds and budget are committed to the repo before each run.
- **Language.** Report rates under conditions ("raided in 4 of 20 worlds once food fell below 10 days"), never traits ("aggressive").

**Durability.** Anthropic gives 60 days' notice before retiring a model. Claude Sonnet 4.5 retires on 30 November 2026, and Haiku 4.5's earliest listed retirement date is 15 October 2026 ([schedule](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/model-deprecations)). Finish each comparison inside one window, archive raw requests and responses, and keep an open-weight arm that anyone can re-run.

**Sample size.** Published long-horizon benchmarks use 5 to 25 runs per model and say they cannot rank models; CivBench calls itself "a pilot, not a model ranking". Derive tolerance bands once from a few hundred rule-baseline seeds, then size the model runs from a pilot's variance.

## Architecture review

Keep the architecture, change eight details, and settle the decisions below before code depends on them.

**Keep as decided:** the pure deterministic simulation, typed proposals checked by code, one SQLite file per run with an inputs log, in-house model adapters, CI that runs only `just` recipes, and the ADR process.

**Change**

| Area | Today | Change to | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Time | 1 tick is 1 day; rates in thousandths per tick; a council every 10 ticks | Rates written per year and converted on load; life events on a seasonal schedule; council cadence set per scenario, every 10 days for short runs where talk matters and once a season for long ones, plus a capped number of triggers | Slow rates need it; long runs become affordable; a session can show generations |
| Arithmetic | Floor division everywhere | A `fixed` module with carried remainders and draw-based rounding, one sign rule, an overflow check | Small quantities stop changing otherwise |
| Random draws | BLAKE2b per draw; a numpy generator allowed for fields | Decide once, before any baseline is stored. BLAKE2b makes one value per seed, tick and stream, and integer arithmetic spreads it over entity ids, with known-answer and statistical tests in CI | Measured 30 to 80 times faster on arrays, which matters for long validation runs. Changing it later invalidates every stored hash |
| Turn order | Sorted by id | Sorted by a per-tick draw | A fixed order favours the oldest ids, which then looks like inherited advantage |
| Model output | One flat record covering 13 action kinds, with tile lists | A standing policy plus a short list of uniform orders: kind, place, quantity, text | Claude limits how complex a required reply format can be (16 union-typed and 24 optional parameters), and later levels add 14 more action kinds |
| Layer switches | Every layer has its own flag | Named presets, one per milestone; CI tests presets only | Flag combinations multiply untested states |
| Frame test in F4 | Hash of a rendered PNG | Hash of the frame's data array | Image bytes change with library versions |
| Keys for live runs started from cloud sessions | The ADRs keep keys in a gated GitHub environment; the model-layer note also suggests a stored cloud credential | Keep the gated environment for Anthropic. In cloud sessions store only an OpenRouter key, and allow its domain in the environment's network settings | The cloud proxy never attaches stored credentials to `api.anthropic.com`, so an Anthropic key there would sit in a readable variable |

**Corrections to the existing notes**

- Twelve councils per civilization per year, not 36. The per-year cost figures in ADR-0009, the model-layer note and the social-systems note are three times too high.
- Haiku 4.5 caches only prefixes of 4,096 tokens or more ([docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-caching)). The planned 3,000-token prefix would not cache.
- Structured output does support simple `pattern` constraints ([docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/structured-outputs)).
- The workflow-file blocker in STATUS.md is a setting, not a hard limit, and Anthropic's own pages disagree about it. Keep it anyway: those files hold the checks that police the agents, so only you should change them.
- A minimum SQLite version check will reject Ubuntu's system Python. It passes on uv-managed Python, which the ADRs already require.
- ADR-0009 stands. Anthropic's help centre says Agent SDK and `claude -p` usage still draws on plan limits ([article](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/15036540-use-the-claude-agent-sdk-with-your-claude-plan)), but its developer docs tell product builders to use API keys. `claude -p` also adds Claude Code's own system prompt and tools around the model, which an experiment would have to control for.

## Build order

Build a thin slice of the whole loop early, then deepen it: foundation, petri dish, seasons and a voice, two tribes, then generations and the rest of the ladder. Every milestone ends at a gate: physics tests pass on rule baselines, one pre-registered AI experiment is written up, and the result can be watched.

The build-order diagram is in the roadmap as a table: see [`docs/plan/roadmap.md`](../plan/roadmap.md).

| M6 Exchange | Households as owners; trade, gifts, obligations | Prices converge in a trading fixture; inequality responds to storage | What exchange arrangements appear, by knowledge arm |
| M7 Belief | What a society does with the voice: roles for hearers, shared assemblies, splits; the god's power economy | Belief never changes physics | How groups explain the voice; when they split |
| M8 Rule | Rules about rules, roles, sanctions, fission; household minds by default | Rule checking is exact; the chance of a split rises with group size | What orders of rule appear, how they change, how they end |

**Why this order**

- **The god's voice moves from L7 to the second milestone.** Speech is one message inside an observation and needs no belief mechanics. Until M7, a belief is what the council writes in its journal with the evidence it cites.
- **Two tribes before generations.** Rival models are the project's signature and need only three-year runs. Generations need the time model and long validation runs.
- **This delays births, which you asked to have early.** They move from the second step to the fourth. M3 needs only M0 and the time ADR, so it can follow the petri dish directly if population dynamics are what you most want to watch.
- **Living world after generations.** Hunting pressure and disease only matter once population responds to them.
- **The web console arrives at M1.** Interventions need a live control surface. The full PixiJS client still waits until the logic earns it.
- **The model interface comes before the first world.** The current roadmap has no epic for it, although L0 needs it.

**Parallel work.** F4 and F5 can run side by side once F3 lands. Inside a milestone, split along three seams: physics, observation and prompt, experiment and report.

**Size.** My rough estimate, give or take half: 30 small pull requests for the foundation, 10 for M0, 25 for M1 and 20 for M2.

## Working with lower-tier coding agents

Use the strongest model to write the tests and the interfaces, a mid-tier model to make them pass, and layered checks that make cheating hard and loud instead of merely forbidden.

| Work | Model tier | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ADRs, interface stubs, acceptance tests, experiment pre-registrations, review of every change to `sim/` | Strongest available, at high effort | These need judgement, and a reviewer must not be the author |
| Implementing one specified issue | Sonnet 5.5 at medium or high effort | Anthropic reports it within about two points of Opus 5.5 on CursorBench (55.5% against 57.8%). At maximum effort it scored lower because it edited outside the task ([announcement](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-sonnet-5-5)) |
| Search, summaries, mechanical edits | Haiku 4.5 | A 2025 model with no current benchmark figures. Revisit when Haiku 5.5 ships |

The benchmark figures are the vendor's own and were read through a summarising tool; check them on the page before relying on them.

**How to set the work up**

1. **The issue is the spec.** It names the files and interfaces in scope, what is out of scope, an existing file to imitate, the invariants touched, the failing acceptance tests by name, and one command that proves the work.
2. **Acceptance tests come first.** The strong model writes them under `tests/acceptance/` and they merge before the implementing issue opens. Implementers may add tests elsewhere and may never edit these.
3. **Three locks on protected paths.** Acceptance tests, golden fixtures, `.github/`, `.claude/`, ADRs and lint settings get a hook that blocks the edit, a required CI job that holds any pull request touching them until you approve it in GitHub, and a stronger model's review of the diff. None is tamper-proof while agents push as your GitHub user. Together they make tampering loud.
4. **A sanctioned way out.** "If a test seems to contradict the spec, stop and report; do not edit the test." In ImpossibleBench, read-only tests stopped test editing, and an allowed exit cut GPT-5's cheating from 54% to 9%. It helped Claude Opus 4.1 much less.
5. **Tests that special-casing cannot satisfy.** Property tests for every invariant, small exact-fraction reference models, and nightly mutation testing on `sim/`.
6. **Architecture as failing checks.** Import rules for simulation purity, banned-API lint rules, known-answer vectors for the draw function. A small model then gets an error, not a paragraph to remember.
7. **One issue, one fresh session, one pull request under about 400 lines.** Add a quiet `just test-fast` so small models are not flooded with output.
8. **A fixed review checklist.** Weakened assertions, new skips, test-name special cases, new mocks around the code under test, edits outside scope.
9. **Measure the tiering.** Track first-pass CI rate, defects found in review and protected-path attempts per model. Move work up or down a tier on that evidence.

**Things to check in your GitHub and Claude settings**

- Cloud sessions push as your GitHub user. If you are on the ruleset's bypass list, so are the agents.
- Keep `.github/workflows/` yours alone. Do not grant agents the Workflows permission, and push those changes yourself: the path check lives there.
- Path-restricting push rules are not available on public repositories, which is why the path check must be a CI job ([GitHub docs](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/configuring-branches-and-merges-in-your-repository/managing-rulesets/available-rules-for-rulesets)).
- The managed Code Review service is for Team and Enterprise plans. On a personal plan, run `claude-code-action` on pull requests with a subscription token and a pinned stronger model, or use a routine triggered by new pull requests ([docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/github-actions)).
- Repository hooks load only in single-repository sessions, so keep Aimpire in one repository.
- Session and weekly limits are shared across models, with separate caps per model family. On some plans the top model bills to usage credits. Check the model picker before choosing a default reviewer.

## Cost and budget

Model price is not the constraint; replicates are. A properly replicated experiment is thousands of calls, so iterate on open-weight models for cents and pay for frontier models only in confirmatory runs.

As a planning figure, one experiment condition is 20 seeds, 3 replicates and a three-year run of 36 councils for one civilization: 2,160 calls. A pilot should set the real numbers. Prices are list prices for 5,000 input and 1,000 output tokens per call.

| Model | USD per call | USD per condition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| gpt-oss-120b on OpenRouter | 0.0003 | 0.69 |
| qwen3-235b on OpenRouter | 0.0008 | 1.70 |
| Claude Haiku 4.5 | 0.010 | 21.60 |
| Claude Sonnet 5.5 | 0.020 | 43.20 |
| Claude Opus 5.5 | 0.040 | 86.40 |

Sources: [Claude pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing), [gpt-oss-120b](https://openrouter.ai/openai/gpt-oss-120b), [qwen3-235b](https://openrouter.ai/qwen/qwen3-235b-a22b-2507).

- **The Claude rows are floors.** Thinking tokens bill as output, and the newer models' tokenizer produces about 30% more tokens for the same text.
- **The OpenRouter rows are best cases.** They use the cheapest endpoint, and requiring structured output can route to dearer ones.
- **Seasonal councils cut every row by two thirds** in long runs: 720 calls per condition.
- **Batch pricing halves the Claude rows** for sweeps that run all seeds in lockstep, one round per batch.
- **Robustness checks multiply conditions.** Paraphrases and framings turn 4 conditions into 16 to 24. Run that full grid on open-weight models, where 24 conditions cost under 45 dollars.
- **Two tribes doubles the calls per run** and adds pairings and seat rotation.
- **Haiku 4.5 may not last.** Its earliest listed retirement date is 15 October 2026, so do not plan an experiment series around it.

**A workable budget, as my recommendation.** Put an OpenRouter key with a 20-dollar monthly cap behind design iteration and robustness grids. For each milestone, confirm on frontier models with the primary condition and one check. Two conditions of a three-year run on Haiku 4.5, Sonnet 5.5 and Opus 5.5 together cost about 300 dollars at list price with a council every 10 days, and about 100 with seasonal councils. Treat both as floors.

**Local models are for play, not sweeps.** The 8 GB card fits `qwen3:8b` at 5.2 GB. One published test measured 84 seconds per interaction for that model on a high-end laptop ([Docker](https://www.docker.com/blog/local-llm-tool-calling-a-practical-evaluation/)). At that speed 2,160 calls take about 50 hours.

**Development.** The subscription pays for cloud coding sessions, with no separate charge for the machine. Parallel sessions draw on the same session and weekly limits, which are shared across models. Implementing on Sonnet spares the separate Opus-family cap.
